The primary rule uses a divergence rate rather than a count, because a count tightens as you sample more and is therefore not a rule. Every slice is also scored under the stricter zero-divergence rule so the effect of that choice is visible rather than asserted.

## What the grid shows

**Fitting the right functional form made the answer worse.** The matched arm — the one handed the shape that generated the data — reaches higher coverage (1.00 against 0.60) and is nonetheless 10 times further from the truth, with a median relative error of 8.56 against 0.83 and intervals 6 times wider. It has more parameters to identify — a decay and a delay, a slope and a half-saturation point — from the same weak signal, so it pins none of them down and covers the truth by being unable to exclude anything. This is the clearest argument in the project against reading coverage on its own, and it points the opposite way from the obvious expectation that the correctly-specified model should win.

**And it was harder to fit at all.** The matched arm passed its sampler diagnostics in 6 of 20 runs against 15 of 20 for the misspecified one. The extra parameters do not only widen the interval; they make the posterior geometry hard enough that the sampler often cannot explore it. A practitioner who reached for the more flexible model would spend the time and end up with fewer usable fits.

**More data made coverage worse.** For the misspecified model at high collinearity, going from 85 to 156 weeks moved coverage from 0.90 to 0.40 while the intervals narrowed from 7.50 to 4.38. That is what a misspecified model does with more evidence: it becomes more confident about the wrong value. Length helps a model that could have been right and hurts one that could not — which is worth knowing before waiting a year to collect more history.
